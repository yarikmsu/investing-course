## Lesson 7.1: Understanding Investment Risk

### Types of Risk

```
Market Risk (Systematic Risk):
├── Affects the entire market
├── Cannot be eliminated by diversification
├── Examples: recession, pandemic, geopolitics
└── Compensated by: expected returns

Specific Risk (Unsystematic Risk):
├── Affects a specific company/industry
├── Eliminated through diversification
├── Examples: company bankruptcy, scandal
└── Not compensated (no risk premium)
```

Market risk is not the only one. A long-horizon investor faces three
more risks that are easy to overlook:

```
Inflation risk (loss of purchasing power):
├── Cash and nominal bonds lose real value over time
├── At 3% inflation, cash "under the mattress" loses ~26% in 10 years
└── Mitigation: stocks, TIPS, I-bonds

Longevity risk:
├── The risk of outliving your portfolio (running out of money)
├── Most acute in retirement (see sequence-of-returns risk, Lesson 7.6)
└── Mitigation: don't over-allocate to cash, set a sane withdrawal rate

Liquidity risk:
├── The risk of not being able to sell quickly at a fair price
├── Affects real estate, private businesses, exotic funds
└── Mitigation: emergency fund + avoid illiquid holdings
```

### Measuring Risk

```
Standard Deviation:
├── Measure of volatility
├── Higher σ = higher risk
└── S&P 500: σ ≈ 15-20% annually

Beta:
├── Sensitivity to market
├── β = 1: like the market
├── β > 1: more volatile than market
├── β < 1: more stable than market
└── β < 0: inverse correlation

Maximum Drawdown:
├── Maximum decline from peak
├── S&P 500 in 2000-2002: -49%
├── S&P 500 in 2008: -57%
├── S&P 500 in 2020: -34%
└── Important for understanding real risk

Two separate dimensions of risk:
├── Depth — how far the market falls (%)
├── Duration — how long until it returns to the prior peak
└── Duration is what usually breaks investors
    (see 2000-2002 below: ~7 years back to the prior high)
```

### Risk vs Volatility

```
Important distinction:

Volatility — short-term price fluctuations
├── High volatility ≠ high risk
├── For long-term investors — irrelevant
└── Can create opportunities

Real risk:
├── Permanent loss of capital
├── Buying at inflated prices
├── Selling at the bottom due to panic
└── Investing in companies that go bankrupt
```

## Lesson 7.2: Diversification

### Why Diversification Works

```
"The only free lunch in investing"
                          — Harry Markowitz

Principle:
├── Different assets move differently
├── When one falls, another may rise
├── Overall portfolio risk decreases
└── Risk-adjusted return improves (volatility falls)
```

### Levels of Diversification

```
1. Within asset class
   └── Many stocks instead of a few

2. Across asset classes
   ├── Stocks
   ├── Bonds
   ├── Real estate
   └── Commodities

3. By geography
   ├── USA
   ├── Developed markets
   └── Emerging markets

4. By sectors
   ├── Technology
   ├── Financials
   ├── Healthcare
   └── etc.

5. By time
   └── Dollar-cost averaging
```

### How Many Stocks Do You Need?

```
Research shows:

1-2 stocks:   █████████████████████████ Very high risk
5 stocks:     ████████████████████░░░░░ High risk
10 stocks:    █████████████████░░░░░░░░ Medium risk
20 stocks:    ████████████████░░░░░░░░░ Substantially reduced
30 stocks:    ███████████████░░░░░░░░░░ Close to market risk
100+ stocks:  ██████████████░░░░░░░░░░░ Market risk

Conclusion: ETF on the entire market = optimal diversification
```

### Asset Correlation

**Correlation** = how much assets move together

| Value | Interpretation | Diversification |
|-------|----------------|-----------------|
| +1.0 | Perfectly together | ❌ None |
| 0.0 | Independent | ✅ Good |
| -1.0 | Opposite | ✅ Ideal (rare) |

**Example correlations:**

| Asset Pair | Correlation | Level | Implication |
|------------|-------------|-------|-------------|
| US Large Cap ↔ US Small Cap | ~0.85 | 🔴 High | Weak diversification |
| US Stocks ↔ International | ~0.70 | 🟡 Moderate | Some diversification |
| Stocks ↔ Bonds | ~0.20 | 🟢 Low | Good diversification |
| Stocks ↔ Gold | ~0.05 | 🟢 Almost none | Excellent diversification |
| Stocks ↔ Cash | ~0.00 | 🟢 None | Ideal diversification |

> ⚠️ **Correlations are not constant.** The figures above are long-run
> averages, not stable properties. In inflationary regimes the stock-bond
> correlation can turn positive (as in 2022 — see Lesson 7.7), and the two
> fall together. In liquidity crises most risk assets fall at once
> (correlations approach +1). Diversification cushions but does NOT remove
> systematic (market) risk.

### False Diversification

```
Mistakes:

❌ 10 tech stocks = diversification
   └── All in one sector!

❌ SPY + VOO + IVV = diversification
   └── All track S&P 500!

❌ 5 different banks = diversification
   └── All in one industry!

✓ Proper diversification:
  ├── Different asset classes
  ├── Different sectors
  └── Different geographies
```

## Lesson 7.3: Asset Allocation

### Importance of Allocation

```
Research shows:

What determines portfolio returns:
├── Asset Allocation: ~90%
├── Market Timing: ~5%
└── Stock Selection: ~5%

Conclusion: Asset allocation is the key decision!
```

> 📖 Source: Brinson, Hood, Beebower (1986) "Determinants of Portfolio Performance". Disputed: Ibbotson (2010) showed smaller impact. Core insight holds: allocation matters more than stock picking.

### Strategic Allocation

```
Long-term target allocation:

Conservative:
├── Stocks: 30%
├── Bonds: 60%
└── Cash: 10%

Moderate:
├── Stocks: 60%
├── Bonds: 35%
└── Cash: 5%

Aggressive:
├── Stocks: 90%
├── Bonds: 10%
└── Cash: 0%
```

### Determining Risk Profile

```
Answer the questions:

1. How long will you invest?
   ├── <5 years: Conservative
   ├── 5-15 years: Moderate
   └── >15 years: Aggressive

2. How would you react to a 30% drop?
   ├── Sell everything: Conservative
   ├── Wait: Moderate
   └── Buy more: Aggressive

3. Does your life depend on this money?
   ├── Yes, it's all I have: Conservative
   ├── Have an emergency fund: Moderate
   └── Have other income sources: Aggressive
```

### Risk Capacity vs Tolerance vs Need

The questions above blend three DISTINCT concepts. Professionals keep them separate:

```
Risk capacity:
├── Your OBJECTIVE ability to absorb a loss
├── Driven by: time horizon, income stability, other assets
└── Example: 30 years to retirement + steady paycheck = high

Risk tolerance:
├── Your EMOTIONAL/psychological willingness to ride out drops
├── Driven by temperament, experience, how well you sleep
└── Example: you panic at -20% = low

Risk need:
├── The return you ACTUALLY require to reach your goal
├── Driven by goal size and capital already accumulated
└── Example: goal is nearly funded = low
```

> 💡 **The binding constraint is the LOWER of capacity and tolerance.**
> A retiree with high tolerance but low capacity (no income besides the
> portfolio) should still invest conservatively. A young saver with high
> capacity but low tolerance risks selling at the bottom — they need a
> portfolio they can live with, not the "optimal" one.
>
> If your risk need is LOW (the goal is already nearly funded), taking
> on more risk than you need is itself a risk: you are gambling a result
> you have already secured for a reward you do not need.

```
Key question for self-analysis:
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  "If tomorrow your portfolio dropped 30%,                  │
│   what would you REALLY do?"                               │
│                                                            │
│   A) Sell everything, this is too scary                    │
│   B) Worry, but stay invested                              │

└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

```

## Lesson 7.4: Investment Psychology

### Cognitive Biases

```
Loss Aversion:
├── Pain from losing $100 > joy from winning $100
├── Leads to: selling at bottom, holding losers
└── Solution: automation, long-term plan

Recency Bias:
├── Overweighting recent events
├── "Market grew 10 years, will grow forever"
├── Or: "Market dropped, need to sell"
└── Solution: study market history

Confirmation Bias:
├── Seeking information confirming our opinion
├── Ignoring contradicting facts
└── Solution: deliberately seek counterarguments

Overconfidence:
├── "I'm smarter than the market"
├── "I feel where the market will go"
└── Solution: statistics show the opposite

Herd Mentality:
├── Buy when everyone buys (at top)
├── Sell when everyone sells (at bottom)
└── Solution: stick to your plan
```

### Investor Emotional Cycle

```
                    ⬆ Euphoria (market top)
                   /         \
              Greed         Anxiety
                /              \
           Optimism           Denial
              /                  \
        Hope                   Fear
            /                     \
       Relief                  Panic
            \                    /
             \              Capitulation
              \               ↓ (market bottom)
               \_____________/

Typical behavior:
├── Buying at euphoria (expensive)
└── Selling at capitulation (cheap)

Correct behavior:
├── Buying at fear (cheap)
└── Holding at euphoria (or selling)
```

### How to Control Emotions

```
1. Have a written plan
   ├── Target allocation
   ├── Rebalancing rules
   └── What to do during drops

2. Automate investments
   ├── Automatic contributions
   ├── Automatic reinvestment
   └── Removes emotional decisions

3. Don't check portfolio every day
   ├── Once a month — enough
   ├── Once a quarter — for rebalancing
   └── Less information = less stress

4. Remember history
   ├── Market survived all crises
   ├── Long-term trend is growth
   └── Drops are opportunities

5. Don't follow the crowd
   ├── "When taxi drivers give tips — sell"
   └── "Buy when there's blood in the streets"
```

## Lesson 7.5: Practical Risk Management Strategies

### Emergency Fund

```
Before investing:
├── 3-6 months of expenses in cash
├── In high-yield savings account
├── NOT invested in stocks
└── Protection from selling at wrong time

Size it to your income stability (it's a risk control,
not a fixed number):
├── Two stable W-2 incomes: ~3 months is enough
├── Single / self-employed / commission income: 6-12 months
└── In retirement (drawing down): 1-2+ years of spending in
    cash/short-term bonds — a buffer against sequence-of-
    returns risk (see the Bucket Strategy in Lesson 7.6)
```

### Position Sizing Rules

```
Never invest:
├── Money you'll need in next 3-5 years
├── Your last savings
├── Borrowed money (margin)
└── Money whose loss would be catastrophic

Rules for individual stocks:
├── No more than 5% of portfolio in one stock
├── No more than 20% in one sector
└── Exception: index ETFs
```

### What to Do During a Crisis

```
When market drops 20-40%:

✓ Do:
├── Follow your plan
├── Rebalance (buy stocks)
├── Continue regular investments
├── Use tax-loss harvesting
└── Remember: this is temporary

✗ Don't:
├── Don't panic sell
├── Don't watch news constantly
├── Don't try to guess the bottom
├── Don't listen to "experts" with predictions
└── Don't stop investing
```

### Portfolio Insurance

```
Conservative methods:
├── Higher bond allocation
├── Broader diversification
└── Larger emergency fund

Advanced methods (for experienced):
├── Covered calls (selling call options)
├── Protective puts (insurance with put options)
└── Asset allocation with alternatives

NOT recommended:
├── Constant market timing
├── Stop-losses on long-term positions
└── Complex derivative strategies
```

## Lesson 7.6: Sequence of Returns Risk

### What It Is

```
Order of returns is especially important:
├── When retiring
├── With large regular withdrawals
└── When investing a large sum

With no withdrawals or contributions, order does NOT matter:

Scenario A (growth first):  +30%, +20%, -10%, -5%
Scenario B (decline first): -10%, -5%, +30%, +20%

$100k → $133,380 in BOTH cases
(returns multiply; the order of the factors cannot change the result)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Now add a $5,000 withdrawal at the end of each year
(as in retirement) — and the order becomes critical:

Scenario A (growth first):
Year 1: +30% → $125,000
Year 2: +20% → $145,000
Year 3: -10% → $125,500
Year 4: -5%  → $114,225

Scenario B (decline first):
Year 1: -10% → $85,000
Year 2: -5%  → $75,750
Year 3: +30% → $93,475
Year 4: +20% → $107,170

Difference: $114,225 vs $107,170 — same returns, but bad
years early, combined with withdrawals, eat into capital.
```

> 💡 The key point: with no cash flows, the order of returns is
> irrelevant (see the identical $133,380 above). Sequence-of-returns
> risk appears ONLY with periodic withdrawals (retirement) or
> contributions — that is when the order starts to matter.

### How to Protect Yourself

```
Strategies:
├── Bucket Strategy
│   ├── Bucket 1: Cash for 1-2 years
│   ├── Bucket 2: Bonds for 3-7 years
│   └── Bucket 3: Stocks for 8+ years
│
├── Rising Equity Glide Path
│   └── Increasing stock allocation in retirement
│
└── Flexible Withdrawal Rate
    └── Withdraw less in bad years
```

## Lesson 7.7: Lessons from Past Crises

### Dot-Com Crash 2000-2002: The Longest Recovery

```
What happened:
├── The internet (dot-com) bubble burst
├── Inflated tech valuations collapsed
├── 2001 recession, September 11 attacks
└── A slow, grinding decline over ~2.5 years

Market decline:
├── S&P 500: ~-49% from peak (March 2000 → October 2002)
├── Duration of decline: ~31 months
├── NASDAQ: ~-78% (tech was hit catastrophically)
└── Recovery to peak: the index did not regain its
    2000 high until 2007 (~7 years)

Why this matters more than the depth:
├── The drop was NOT the deepest (2008 was worse)
├── But it was the LONGEST and slowest
├── NASDAQ did not set a new high above 2000 until 2015 (~15 yrs)
└── A long drawdown wears investors down more than a deep one
```

> 💡 Depth and duration are two separate dimensions of risk.
> 2008 was deeper, but 2000-2002 lasted far longer. When you
> set your asset allocation, plan not only for "how far it
> falls" but also for "how many years you may wait to recover."

### 2008-2009 Crisis: The Great Recession

```
What happened:
├── Mortgage crisis in USA
├── Lehman Brothers bankruptcy (September 2008)
├── Global financial crisis
└── Recession in most developed countries

Market decline:
├── S&P 500: -57% from peak (October 2007 → March 2009)
├── Fell from 1565 to 666 points
├── Duration of decline: 17 months
└── Recovery to peak: March 2013 (4 years)

What investors felt:
├── Fear of complete financial system collapse
├── "This time it's different"
├── Mass flight to cash and gold
└── Many sold at bottom and never returned
```

```
If you invested $10,000 in S&P 500 in October 2007 (at peak):

March 2009 (bottom):    $4,300  (-57%)
March 2013 (5 years):   $10,500 (+5%)
October 2017 (10 years): $18,700 (+87%)
October 2022 (15 years): $26,400 (+164%)
January 2025 (17 years): $38,000+ (+280%)

LESSON: Even investing at the VERY PEAK of the worst crisis,
        after 15 years you tripled your money.
```

### COVID Crash 2020: The Fastest Drop and Recovery

```
What happened:
├── COVID-19 pandemic
├── Lockdowns worldwide
├── Economic activity halt
└── Unprecedented Fed support measures

Market decline:
├── S&P 500: -34% in 33 days (fastest in history!)
├── Fell from 3386 to 2237 points
├── February 19, 2020 → March 23, 2020
└── Recovery to peak: August 2020 (5 months!)

Uniqueness:
├── Fastest bear market ever
├── Fastest recovery ever
├── V-shaped recovery
└── By end of 2020, market up 18% for the year
```

```
If you invested $10,000 in S&P 500 February 19, 2020 (at peak):

March 23, 2020 (bottom):   $6,600  (-34%)
August 2020 (6 months):    $10,300 (+3%)
February 2021 (1 year):    $11,800 (+18%)
February 2022 (2 years):   $13,900 (+39%)
January 2025 (5 years):    $15,500+ (+55%)

LESSON: Those who panicked and sold in March 2020,
        missed one of the best rallies in history.
```

### 2022 Bear Market: Inflation and Rates

```
What happened:
├── Inflation reached 9.1% (June 2022) — 40-year high
├── Fed began aggressive rate hikes
├── Rates rose from 0.25% to 5.25% in 18 months
└── End of "cheap money" era

Market decline:
├── S&P 500: -25% (January 2022 → October 2022)
├── NASDAQ: -33% (tech suffered more)
├── BND (bonds): ~-17% by price / -13% total return for the year
└── 60/40 portfolio had worst year since 1937

Uniqueness of 2022:
├── Stocks AND bonds fell simultaneously
├── Diversification "didn't work" (temporarily)
├── Many doubted classic strategies
└── By end of 2023, market nearly recovered (~99% of ATH)
```

#### Why bonds fell: interest-rate risk (duration)

BND's 2022 decline was not bad luck — it was the direct result of
**interest-rate risk**:

```
Duration rule:
price change ≈ −duration × change in rates

BND: effective duration ~6 years
2022: the 10-year Treasury yield rose ~2.7 percentage points
→ −6 × 2.7% ≈ −16-17% (which is what we saw)
```

That is why long-term bonds (TLT, duration ~17 years) fell ~-30%
while short-term bonds barely moved. The second type of bond risk is
**credit (default) risk** — the risk the issuer defaults; it is higher
for corporate and high-yield bonds than for US Treasuries.

> 📖 More on interest-rate and credit risk in [Module 3](/investing-course/en/module-03-instruments/),
> and on BND's duration in [ETF-ANALYSIS](/investing-course/en/ETF-ANALYSIS/).

```
If you invested $10,000 in S&P 500 January 3, 2022 (at peak):

October 2022 (bottom):     $7,500  (-25%)
December 2022 (1 year):    $8,100  (-19%)
December 2023 (2 years):   $10,600 (+6%)
January 2025 (3 years):    $12,000+ (+20%)

LESSON: Even after "non-working diversification" of 2022,
        patient investors came out ahead in 2 years.
```

### Main Lessons

```
1. Crises ALWAYS end
   ├── 2000-2002: recovery took ~7 years
   ├── 2008: recovery took 4 years
   ├── 2020: recovery took 5 months
   ├── 2022: recovery took ~2 years
   └── Every time the market reached new highs
       (but it can take years — see 2000-2002)

2. Impossible to predict the bottom
   ├── In 2009 many waited for "even lower"
   ├── In March 2020 it seemed like just the beginning
   ├── Market turns around when everyone gives up
   └── Trying to catch the bottom, you often miss the rally

3. "This time it's different" — dangerous phrase
   ├── 2008: "Financial system will collapse"
   ├── 2020: "Economy will never recover"
   ├── 2022: "Era of growth is over"
   └── Every time this proved wrong

4. Best days often follow worst
   ├── 6 of 10 best S&P 500 days over 20 years
   │   occurred within 2 weeks of the worst
   ├── Missing 10 best days cuts returns in half
   └── Need to be in market to not miss the rally

5. Patience — the investor's main quality
   ├── $10,000 in 2007 → $38,000+ in 2025
   ├── Through any crisis
   ├── Without trying to guess the market
   └── Simply by staying invested
```

### The Cost of Panic: What the Data Shows

Behavioral losses are not just a story. Per Morningstar's "Mind the Gap"
study (2024 edition, the 10 years ended Dec 31, 2023), the average fund
investor earned ~6.3% per year versus ~7.3% for the funds themselves — a
gap of ~1.1 percentage points per year. The 2025 edition (through Dec 2024)
puts it near ~1.2 pp. The cause is mistimed buying and selling (buying after
gains, selling after drops) — exactly what the illustrations below depict.

> ℹ️ We cite Morningstar's estimate (~1-1.2 pp/yr), NOT the larger DALBAR
> figures (~4-6 pp/yr): DALBAR's QAIB methodology is contested by researchers
> (Pfau; Morningstar's Blanchett) for not properly accounting for cash-flow
> timing. This is educational material, not financial advice.

### Illustrative Examples (Hypothetical)

> ℹ️ Anna, Boris, and Vera are fictional characters. The numbers are for
> illustration only and are not real data.

```
Example 1: Investor Anna (2008)
├── Invested $50,000 in S&P 500 in 2007
├── Panicked in March 2009, sold everything for $21,500
├── "I'll wait until it stabilizes"
├── Returned to market in 2012
├── Result: missed the 2009-2012 rally (+100%)
└── If she had stayed: $190,000+ in 2025

Example 2: Investor Boris (2020)
├── $100,000 in portfolio by February 2020
├── Panicked in March, sold everything for $66,000
├── "COVID will destroy the economy forever"
├── Watched market recover without him
├── Returned in November 2020 (market above peak!)
└── Lost 20%+ due to panic

Example 3: Investor Vera (2022)
├── Continued investing $1,000/month
├── Didn't check portfolio in panic
├── Bought more during the drop
├── Result: lower average purchase price
└── By 2024, portfolio grew 35%
```

### What to Do in the Next Crisis

```
BEFORE the crisis:
☐ Emergency fund 3-6 months
☐ Written investment plan
☐ Automatic investments set up
☐ Allocation matches risk tolerance
☐ Remember history of past crises

DURING the crisis:
☐ Follow plan, don't improvise
☐ Continue regular investments
☐ Rebalance (buy discounted assets)
☐ Limit financial news consumption
☐ Remind yourself: "This is temporary"

AFTER the crisis:
☐ Analyze your emotions and decisions
☐ Adjust plan if needed
☐ Remember the lesson for next time
```

<summary><b>Question 3:</b> What is Loss Aversion and how does it harm investors?</summary>

**Answer:** Loss Aversion — the pain from loss is stronger than joy from equal gain. Leads to selling at the bottom and holding losing positions. Solution: automation and following a plan.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Question 4:</b> What emergency fund size is recommended before investing?</summary>

**Answer:** 3-6 months of expenses in a high-yield savings account. Protects from needing to sell investments at the wrong time.
