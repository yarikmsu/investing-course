## Lesson 5.1: Passive vs Active Investing

### Passive Investing

```
Philosophy:
├── Market is efficient — hard to beat it
├── Low fees matter more than stock picking
├── Time in market > Timing the market
└── "Buy the market and hold"

Instruments:
├── Index funds
├── Broad market ETFs
└── Target-date funds

Advantages:
├── Low fees (0.03-0.20%)
├── Minimal time required
├── Diversification
├── Tax efficiency
└── Statistically beats ~85-90% of active funds over a 15+ year horizon
```

### Active Investing

```
Philosophy:
├── Can beat the market by picking stocks
├── Analysis and research provide an edge
└── Willing to pay for potentially better results

Instruments:
├── Individual stocks
├── Actively managed funds
└── Hedge funds

Advantages:
├── Potential for higher returns
├── Ability to avoid downturns
├── Strategy flexibility
└── Intellectual satisfaction

Disadvantages:
├── High fees (0.5-2.0%+)
├── Requires significant time
├── Most underperform the index
└── Emotional mistakes
```

### What the Statistics Say

```
Over 15 years (S&P Dow Jones Indices data):

Percentage of active funds that underperformed the index:
├── Large-Cap: ~90%
├── Mid-Cap: ~85%
├── Small-Cap: ~90%
└── International: ~80%

Conclusion: For most investors, passive approach is optimal.
```

## Lesson 5.2: Buy and Hold

### Strategy Essentials

```
Principles:
├── Buy quality assets
├── Hold long-term (years, decades)
├── Ignore short-term fluctuations
├── Only sell on fundamental changes
└── Minimize transactions
```

### Advantages

```
Financial:
├── Low transaction costs
├── Tax efficiency (long-term gains)
├── Power of compound interest
└── Dividends reinvested

Psychological:
├── Less stress
├── No need to constantly watch the market
├── Protection from emotional decisions
└── Simplicity
```

### Historical Perspective

```
S&P 500 — NOMINAL US returns (data from 1926):

If you held:
├── 1 year: positive result ~74% of the time
├── 5 years: positive result ~88% of the time
├── 10 years: positive result ~95% of the time
├── 20 years: positive result ~100% of the time (nominal)

Worst 20-year return: ~2-3% annually (nominal, with reinvestment)
Average 20-year return: ~10-11% annually (nominal)
```

> ⚠️ **Important caveats to the "~100% over 20 years" stat.** These figures are NOMINAL
> (not adjusted for inflation) and apply only to the US market over a specific historical
> period.
> - **On an inflation-adjusted (real) basis,** the worst 20-year window was roughly
>   flat — about +0.8% per year (early 1960s start). Positive, but barely growing in
>   purchasing power. So "always positive over 20 years" is a nominal statement; the
>   real outcome could be only marginally above zero.
> - **The US is a single country.** Several other developed markets have had 20+ year
>   stretches of negative real returns. Past US leadership doesn't guarantee a repeat —
>   an argument for global diversification (see Lesson 5.7) and against 100% US stocks
>   for retirement-horizon money.

### When Buy and Hold Does NOT Work

```
Warnings:
├── Individual companies can go bankrupt
├── Entire industries can become obsolete
├── Diversification is mandatory!
├── Don't confuse with "buy and forget"
└── Periodic rebalancing is needed
```

> ⚠️ **100% equities isn't a "free" maximization, even over a long horizon.** An
> all-stock portfolio has the highest expected return but raises two risks: (1)
> **behavioral** — without bonds, investors are more likely to panic and sell at the
> bottom in a deep bear market, locking in the loss (the biggest real-world driver of
> underperformance), and (2) **sequence risk** — a large drawdown early in accumulation,
> or just before you need the money, hurts disproportionately. Even a modest bond sleeve
> (10-20%) is primarily behavioral/risk insurance, not a way to earn more.

## Lesson 5.3: Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA)

### Strategy Essentials

Regular investments of a fixed amount regardless of price.

```
Example:
├── Every month invest $500
├── Price high — buy fewer shares
├── Price low — buy more shares
└── Average purchase price smooths out
```

### How DCA Works

```
Month    Share Price   Amount    Shares Bought
──────────────────────────────────────────────
Jan      $100         $500      5.00
Feb      $80          $500      6.25
Mar      $90          $500      5.56
Apr      $110         $500      4.55
May      $95          $500      5.26
──────────────────────────────────────────────
Total:                $2,500    26.62

Average purchase price: $93.91
Current value at $95: $2,528.90
```

### DCA Advantages

```
├── Reduces impact of volatility
├── Removes emotions from investing
├── No market timing required
├── Disciplines regular investing
├── Suitable for beginners
└── Works automatically
```

### DCA vs Lump Sum

```
Research shows:

Lump Sum (one-time investment):
├── Historically gives better results ~67% of the time
├── Money works longer
└── But: psychologically harder

DCA:
├── Loses to Lump Sum ~33% of the time
├── But protects from bad timing
└── Psychologically more comfortable

Recommendation:
├── Have a large sum → Lump Sum
├── Regular income → DCA
└── Feeling nervous → DCA
```

> ⚠️ **Key clarification.** If you already hold the money (an inheritance, a bonus,
> proceeds from a sale), investing it all at once (lump sum) is the
> expected-value-maximizing default: historically lump sum has beaten DCA about
> **two-thirds of the time**, by a modest average margin (~2.3 percentage points
> of the amount over a one-year deployment, per Vanguard) — you trade that small
> edge for psychological comfort. DCA-ing a lump sum
> is a behavioral safeguard against your own panic, not a way to earn more. And
> contributing from each paycheck isn't a "DCA vs lump sum" decision at all —
> you're simply investing money as you earn it, because you don't yet hold the
> full sum.

### 💡 Detailed Example: DCA vs Lump Sum

You have **$6,000** to invest. Two options:

**Option A: Lump Sum** — invest everything at once

**Option B: DCA** — $500/month for a year

```
Scenario 1: Market rises all year (+10%)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Lump Sum: $6,000 × 1.10 = $6,600 (+$600)
DCA: Average price higher → ~$6,330 (+$330)
Winner: Lump Sum
```

```
Scenario 2: Market falls all year (-10%)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Lump Sum: $6,000 × 0.90 = $5,400 (-$600)
DCA: Average price lower → ~$5,700 (-$300)
Winner: DCA (fewer losses)
```

```
Scenario 3: Volatile market (V-shaped)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Falls -20% in first 6 months, then +25%
Lump Sum: $6,000 × 0.80 × 1.25 = $6,000 (no change)
DCA: Buy cheap in middle → ~$6,450 (+$450)
Winner: DCA
```

> 📊 **Main takeaway:** DCA wins in falling and volatile markets, Lump Sum — in rising markets. Since markets rise more often, Lump Sum statistically wins in ~67% of cases.

### 🎯 Psychological Advantage of DCA

```
Why DCA is popular despite the statistics:

1. Reduces fear of "buying at the top"
   └── Worst-case scenario is spread over months

2. Automates discipline
   └── Money goes out before you can spend it

3. Removes choice paralysis
   └── No need to decide "when to enter"

4. Emotionally more comfortable
   └── Market drops = discount, not disaster

5. Compatible with salary
   └── Most people receive income monthly
```

### 📈 Accumulation with Regular Investing

Investing **$500/month** in S&P 500 (historically ~10% annually):

| Timeframe | Invested | Total at 10% | Profit |
|-----------|----------|--------------|--------|
| 5 years | $30,000 | ~$39,000 | +$9,000 |
| 10 years | $60,000 | ~$103,000 | +$43,000 |
| 20 years | $120,000 | ~$380,000 | +$260,000 |
| 30 years | $180,000 | **~$1,130,000** | +$950,000 |

> 💰 **$500/month could grow to roughly a million over 30 years** — thanks to compound
> interest. But this is an illustration, not a promise.

> ⚠️ **Two important caveats.**
> - **10% is a historical US market AVERAGE**, not a guarantee. The actual path depends
>   on the sequence of returns (sequence risk): two portfolios with the same average
>   return but a different order of good and bad years end up at different values.
> - **This is a nominal figure.** Adjusted for ~2.5% annual inflation, ~$1.13M in 30
>   years is roughly **$540k in today's dollars** — about half as much in purchasing
>   power. Plan your goals in real (inflation-adjusted) terms.

## Lesson 5.4: Dividend Investing

### Strategy Essentials

Focus on companies that regularly pay and increase dividends.

```
Goal:
├── Stable passive income
├── Dividend growth over time
└── Total return = Dividends + Price appreciation
```

### Dividend Aristocrats

S&P 500 companies that have increased dividends for 25+ consecutive years.

```
Examples (as of writing):
├── Johnson & Johnson (JNJ) — 60+ years
├── Coca-Cola (KO) — 60+ years
├── Procter & Gamble (PG) — 60+ years
├── 3M (MMM) — 60+ years
├── Walmart (WMT) — 50+ years
└── McDonald's (MCD) — 45+ years
```

### Key Metrics

```
Dividend Yield:
├── Formula: (Annual Dividend / Price) × 100%
├── Good yield: 2-4%
├── Very high (>6%): may be risky
└── Low (<2%): may grow faster

Payout Ratio:
├── Formula: Dividends / Earnings × 100%
├── Healthy: 40-60%
├── High (>80%): risk of cuts
└── Low (<30%): growth potential

Dividend Growth Rate:
├── How much % it increases annually
└── 5-10% growth — excellent
```

### DRIP (Dividend Reinvestment Plan)

```
Automatic dividend reinvestment:
├── Dividends buy additional shares
├── Accelerates compound growth
├── No purchase commissions
└── Works automatically

Example over 30 years:
├── Initial investment: $10,000
├── Without DRIP (7% growth): ~$76,000
├── With DRIP (7% growth + 3% yield): ~$170,000
```

### Dividend ETFs

| Ticker | Name | Yield | Focus |
|--------|------|-------|-------|
| VYM | Vanguard High Dividend | ~3% | High dividends |
| SCHD | Schwab US Dividend | ~3.5% | Quality + dividends |
| VIG | Vanguard Dividend Appreciation | ~2% | Dividend growth |
| NOBL | ProShares S&P 500 Aristocrats | ~2.5% | Dividend Aristocrats |
| DGRO | iShares Core Dividend Growth | ~2.5% | Dividend growth |

> ℹ️ Yields are approximate (as of early 2025) — historical/indicative figures; verify current values before deciding.

## Lesson 5.5: Growth vs Value Investing

### 📊 Growth vs Value Comparison

| Characteristic | Growth | Value |
|----------------|--------|-------|
| **Multiples** | High P/E, P/S | Low P/E, P/B |
| **Dividends** | Minimal / none | Often pay |
| **Volatility** | High | Low |
| **Earnings growth** | Fast | Stable |
| **Typical sectors** | Tech, biotech | Finance, energy |
| **Examples** | NVDA, TSLA, AMZN | JPM, JNJ, BRK |

### Growth Investing

```
Growth stock characteristics:
├── High revenue/earnings growth rates
├── Reinvest profits into development
├── Minimal or no dividends
├── High multiples (P/E, P/S)
└── More volatile

Typical sectors:
├── Technology
├── Biotechnology
├── E-commerce
└── Renewable energy

Examples:
├── Amazon (until recently)
├── Tesla
├── NVIDIA
└── Salesforce
```

### Value Investing

```
Value stock characteristics:
├── Trade below "fair" value
├── Low multiples (P/E, P/B)
├── Stable business
├── Often pay dividends
└── Less volatile

Typical sectors:
├── Financials
├── Energy
├── Consumer staples
└── Telecommunications

Examples:
├── Berkshire Hathaway
├── JPMorgan Chase
├── Johnson & Johnson
└── Exxon Mobil
```

### Historical Cycles

```
Growth vs Value (historical trends):

1990-2000: Growth dominates (tech boom)
2000-2007: Value dominates (tech bust)
2007-2009: Both fall (financial crisis)
2009-2020: Growth dominates (low rates + tech)
2022: Value outperforms (rising rates)
2023-2024: Growth returns (AI boom)

Conclusion: Cycles alternate, hold both!
```

### Blend Approach

```
Recommendation for most:
├── Use broad index funds
├── They contain both growth and value
├── Don't try to guess the cycle
└── Let the market determine the proportions

Example:
├── VTI (Total Stock Market) — contains everything
└── S&P 500 — balance of growth and value
```

## Lesson 5.6: Factor Investing

### Main Factors

```
Size:
├── Small companies > Large (historically)
└── ETFs: VB, IWM (small cap)

Value:
├── Cheap stocks > Expensive (historically)
└── ETFs: VTV, IUSV

Momentum:
├── Rising stocks continue to rise
└── ETF: MTUM

Quality:
├── Profitable, stable companies
└── ETF: QUAL

Low Volatility:
├── Less volatile stocks
└── ETFs: USMV, SPLV
```

> ⚠️ **Factors are not a "free lunch."** The historical size and value premia are not
> guaranteed: the standalone size premium has been weak and insignificant out of sample,
> and value endured a drawdown of more than a decade (roughly 2007-2020). These are
> optional tilts — a bet on additional risk whose premium isn't guaranteed, not reliable outperformance. One
> important detail: to actually capture these factors you need small-cap *value*
> specifically (e.g. AVUV), since plain small-cap funds like VB or IWM don't deliver the
> size premium on their own. For most investors, a broad index fund (VTI) is more than
> enough.

### Multifactor ETFs

```
Examples:
├── VFMF — Vanguard U.S. Multifactor
├── LRGF — iShares U.S. Equity Factor
└── Combine multiple factors
```

## Lesson 5.7: Global Diversification (VXUS / VEA / VWO)

### 🌍 Why hold non-US stocks

```
The US is ~60% of the global stock market — not 100%.
Over the past decade US stocks have far outpaced the rest of the world,
but that has NOT always been the case:

├── 2000-2009: international stocks beat the US (the S&P 500's "lost decade")
├── 2010-2024: the US led decisively
└── No one knows whose turn is next

Practical takeaway:
├── An ex-US allocation of 20-40% of stocks reduces single-country risk
├── Simple route: VT (whole world) or VTI + VXUS
└── Don't mistake recent performance for a guarantee of future returns (recency bias)
```

> ⚠️ **The neutral point is the market, not "100% US."** By market capitalization, the
> world is roughly 60% US and 40% ex-US (that's exactly how VT is built). So a 20-25%
> ex-US allocation (as in the classic three-fund VTI 60% / VXUS 20%) is not a neutral
> default — it's a deliberate US home-country tilt. That's a legitimate choice, but it's
> a bet that the US keeps outperforming the rest of the world, not a "safe middle."

> 💡 Past results don't predict future returns — and for individual countries
> that's especially true. Global diversification is insurance, not a bet.

### 💱 Currency Risk

If you spend and measure your savings in a currency other than US dollars, you carry
an extra source of risk: exchange-rate risk. Any result has to be evaluated in your
home currency, not in dollars.

```
Why it matters:
├── A portfolio can be up +10% in US dollars
│   yet be flat or down in your home currency if that currency strengthened
├── And vice versa: if your home currency weakens, a USD portfolio "grows" in it
└── Over short horizons, currency swings can outweigh the stocks' return
```

> ⚠️ **Key nuance: the risk depends on the currency of the UNDERLYING holdings, not the
> currency the ETF trades in.** A USD ticker by itself does not create the risk. What
> matters is what the companies and bonds inside the fund are denominated in:
> - **VOO, VTI, BND** — almost everything inside is in US dollars → for a non-USD
>   investor this is effectively a "home currency vs USD" exposure.
> - **VT, VXUS** — about 40% of the holdings are outside the US (yen, euro, pound,
>   etc.) → the exposure is to a basket of world currencies, not purely USD.
>
> Takeaway: a global fund (VT) incidentally diversifies some currency risk too, but
> doesn't remove it. Think about returns in your own currency.
